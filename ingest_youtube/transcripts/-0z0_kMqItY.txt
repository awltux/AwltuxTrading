Chapter 7 How to research stocks, fundamental analysis. Once you move beyond pure index fund investing and want to start evaluating individual companies, you need a framework for understanding whether a stock is actually worth buying at its current price. The most common approach is called fundamental analysis, which means looking at the actual health and financial performance of the underlying business, not just watching the stock price chart. Revenue and net income. Revenue is the total amount of money a company brings in from selling its products or services before any costs are subtracted. Net income, also called profit or the bottom line, is what remains after subtracting all expenses, cost of goods sold, salaries, rent, marketing, interest payments, and taxes. These are the two most basic measures of a business's health. What you want to see is both of these growing over time. Consistent revenue growth shows the business is expanding its market. Consistent net income growth shows it is becoming more efficient and profitable as it scales. You can find historical revenue and net income numbers on financial sites like Yahoo Finance, Morningstar, Macrotrends, or directly on the e-company's investor relations page in their quarterly earnings reports. The price-to-earnings ratio. The PE ratio is probably the most widely used valuation metric in all [music] of investing. It tells you how much investors are currently paying for every dollar of a company's annual earnings. If a company earns $5 per share in profit over the last 12 months and its stock currently costs $100 per share, its PE ratio is 20. That means investors are paying $20 for each dollar of current earnings. Essentially paying a premium based on the expectation that the company will continue to grow and its earnings will be much higher in the future. A higher PE ratio suggests investors expect strong future growth. A lower PE might mean the stock is undervalued or simply that growth expectations are modest. The critical thing to understand is that PE ratios only make sense when compared within the same industry. Technology companies routinely trade at PE ratios of 30, 40, 50, or even higher because their growth potential is enormous. Utility companies often trade at PE ratios in the teens or low 20s because their earnings are stable but grow slowly. Comparing the PE of a tech startup to the PE of a regulated utility company is completely meaningless. It is apples and oranges. The price-to-sales ratio. The PS ratio compares a company's market capitalization to its total annual revenue. It is particularly useful when analyzing companies that do not yet have positive earnings, which is common among younger, faster growing businesses that are investing heavily in expansion at the expense of near-term profitability. You cannot calculate a meaningful PE ratio for a company losing money, but you can ask whether the price you are paying is reasonable relative to the scale of its business based on revenue. A low PS ratio compared to industry peers might suggest a stock is undervalued. A very high PS ratio means investors are pricing in enormous future growth that the company needs to actually deliver to justify the valuation. Free cash flow Free cash flow is the amount of actual real cash a company generates after covering all its operating expenses and necessary capital investments, such as buying new equipment or upgrading facilities. It is different from accounting profit because accounting rules allow things like depreciation and amortization that affect reported profits without representing [music] actual cash moving in or out. Free cash flow is often described as the truest measure of a business's financial health because it tells you how much money the company is actually generating that it can use freely. A company with consistently growing free cash flow can reinvest in growth, pay dividends, buy back its own shares to increase shareholder value, or pay down debt. A company with declining or negative free cash flow over multiple years has a problem that needs explaining. And if it cannot be explained satisfactorily, it might be a reason to avoid the stock. Is the company led by its founder or by a hired professional manager? Founder-led companies have historically outperformed because founders have skin in the game, deep institutional knowledge, and often a long-term vision that professional managers focused on quarterly earnings targets can lack. Does the company have a durable competitive advantage, something that makes it very hard for competitors to take its customers. This might be a powerful brand, network effects where the product becomes more valuable as more people use it, proprietary technology protected by patents, high switching costs that make it painful for customers to leave, or cost advantages from massive scale. Taxes are one of the biggest things that separate smart investors from naive ones. Understanding even the basics of investment taxation can save you enormous amounts of money over a lifetime. And making uninformed decisions can cost you just as much. Capital gains taxes. When you sell an investment for more than you paid for it, the profit is called a capital gain. In the United States, capital gains are taxed differently depending on how long you held the investment before selling. Short-term capital gains apply when you sell within 12 months of buying. These gains are taxed as ordinary income at the same rate you pay on your paycheck. Depending on your total income, that could be anywhere from 10% to 37%. Long-term capital gains apply when you sell after holding for more than 12 months. The tax rate is significantly lower. 0% for lower-income earners, 15% for most middle-income households, and 20% for higher earners. The difference in tax rates gives you a powerful incentive to hold investments for at least a year before selling. One important thing to understand, you only owe taxes when you actually [music] sell and realize the gain. If you buy a stock and it doubles in value, but you keep [music] holding it, you have an unrealized gain. No taxes owed. The tax only triggers when you actually sell. This is one reason why long-term buy and hold investing is so tax-efficient. You can compound gains inside your portfolio indefinitely without paying any taxes until you eventually decide to sell. Tax loss harvesting. At the end of each year, many investors look through their portfolio for positions that are currently at a loss. They can sell those positions to realize the loss on paper, use that loss to offset any capital gains they have made elsewhere that year, and thereby reduce their overall tax bill. After selling the losing position, >> [music] >> they can invest the proceeds in something similar, not identical because of a rule called the wash sale rule, to maintain their overall portfolio exposure. Tax loss harvesting does not eliminate taxes forever, but it defers them and puts more money to work compounding in the short term. The power of tax-advantaged accounts. This brings us back to why the Roth IRA and 401k matter so much from a tax perspective. Inside these accounts, you do not owe taxes on dividends received, interest earned, or capital gains realized from buying and selling investments. You can rebalance your portfolio, [music] take profits, and reinvest as often as you want without any immediate tax consequences. >> [music] >> Over a 30- or 40-year investing horizon, the ability to let gains compound without a tax drag every year makes a meaningful difference in your final [music] balance.
